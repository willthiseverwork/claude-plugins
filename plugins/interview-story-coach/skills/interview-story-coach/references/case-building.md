# Building cases and gap answers

## Build a case from evidence, not a template

An interview story earns its place by helping an interviewer make a decision. Start with the role requirement, then recover the smallest concrete event that can support it. A case can be brief, but it should answer these questions:

| Element | What to establish |
| --- | --- |
| Situation and constraint | What needed to change, and why was it difficult? |
| Personal ownership | What did the person decide, diagnose, recommend, or execute? |
| Team ownership | What did other people or the organisation deliver? |
| Mechanism | How did the decision become action? Include meaningful trade-offs. |
| Result | What changed? Keep the metric, comparison, and period only if the person can support them. |
| Learning and relevance | What did it change in their approach, and why does it matter for this role? |
| Boundary | What does this case not prove? |

Ask a question that reveals the next unknown rather than requesting all elements at once. A useful sequence might be: the first signal they noticed; the decision they made; who else was involved; the measure they used; then the result and what they learned.

## Keep evidence categories honest

### Direct experience

The person personally owned a relevant decision or execution. Explain their actual scope; a direct case may still have bounded attribution.

### Bounded experience

The case is useful with a stated limitation. For example, a manager may have led the rollout while another team owned the technical implementation, or a metric may cover one launch period rather than a durable result.

### Adjacent experience

The underlying judgment transfers, but the exact requirement has not been completed. Name both the closest evidence and the material difference.

### Proposed approach

This is a future-facing answer, not evidence that the person has done the work. It should state the first information they would seek, the decision options, the operating constraint, and how they would test progress.

### No evidence yet

Say so plainly. Ask whether there is a different real example, or prepare an honest gap answer if there is not.

## Shape a concise spoken answer

Use this order when it helps:

1. Answer the question directly.
2. Give one case: situation, personal decision, mechanism, and result.
3. State the learning.
4. Tie it to the interviewer’s decision.

Do not label these parts aloud or force every answer into the same length. Stop when the answer is complete.

## Handle a material gap

An honest gap answer normally has five truth units:

1. acknowledge the exact experience that is missing;
2. name the closest real evidence;
3. explain the important difference;
4. offer a first-principles approach without pretending it is a past result; and
5. ask the one question that establishes whether the gap is material.

For example: “I have not owned enterprise procurement for a regulated buyer. My closest experience was changing the onboarding path for a consumer subscription product, where I owned the experiment design and outcome review. The buying process and stakeholders are very different. I would begin by mapping the buyer, user, security, and legal blockers before proposing changes. How much of this role is expected to own the procurement process directly?”

This example is illustrative. Replace every fact with the user's actual experience.
